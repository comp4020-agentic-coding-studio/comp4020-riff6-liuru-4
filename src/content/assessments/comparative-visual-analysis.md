## The brief

> In 600--900 words, set one securely attributed Tang Yin painting beside
> one disputed one and account for what you see, using nothing but your
> eyes and the vocabulary of weeks 1--2.

This is the "two piles" seminar done slowly and in writing. In the room you
sorted six reproductions cold and named the feature that drove each call;
here you take two of them --- one from each pile, and you are told which is
which --- and work out what actually differs. The comparison is the
instrument. A feature you cannot see in the secure painting is much easier
to trust as a difference than one you noticed in the disputed painting and
then went looking to justify.

Write about the paintings, not about the literature. You may well find that
a scholar has already said what you are about to say, and citing them is
fine, but a paragraph summarising an existing argument earns nothing here.
The assessed skill is whether you can look and then say what you saw.

## Why it comes before the toolkit

Every later assessment lets you reach for materials, brushwork, seals,
colophons and the market. This one does not, because the semester's whole
premise is that those instruments refine a judgement the eye makes first,
and a connoisseur who has never had to defend a purely visual reading tends
to hide behind technical evidence later. Week 12 asks you to re-sort the
same six reproductions; this is the baseline that comparison is measured
against.

The third marking criterion is the one students underweight. Naming the
limits of looking --- "these two hands differ in the treatment of foliage,
but I cannot tell from a reproduction whether that is the painter or the
paper" --- is not hedging. It is the beginning of knowing which instrument
to pick up next.

A single markdown or PDF file, 600--900 words, uploaded through the course

any AI or outside research assistance in a short footnote --- see the
